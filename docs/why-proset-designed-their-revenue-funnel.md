# Why Proset Designed Their Revenue Funnel the Way They Did

The mobile application economy has undergone a fundamental transformation, with subscription-based applications becoming the dominant monetization model across platforms (Shang et al., 2024). Yet the freemium model, which dominates application distribution, exhibits persistently low conversion rates: a majority of freemium apps convert only 2% to 5% of users to paid subscribers, while applications employing more restrictive access models achieve materially higher conversion rates (Cao et al., 2023; Shang et al., 2024). This gap represents a systematic design failure in how most freemium applications structure their user experience. Proset, a voice-recording and AI-powered content generation application that captures speech, transcribes it, and converts the resulting text into over 20 distinct artifact types — including summaries, emails, task lists, blog posts, project plans, and calendar events — was redesigned to address these structural failures, grounding every decision in the best available peer-reviewed research on consumer behavior, subscription economics, and freemium monetization.

## Why Not a Fully Locked App? The Hard Paywall Decision

Before determining how to structure the freemium tier, a more fundamental question required resolution: should the application require payment at all? The empirical evidence on hard paywalls — in which users must pay before accessing any functionality — is unambiguous and increasingly compelling. Numminen et al. (2022) examined the impact of revenue model choices on application performance and found that paid–paid models, requiring upfront payment for both download and subsequent features, generated significantly higher per-user revenue than freemium alternatives. The mechanism is straightforward: mandatory payment filters out users with no purchase intent, leaving a cohort of committed subscribers who generate more revenue per install and exhibit lower churn. RevenueCat's 2026 State of Subscription Apps report, analyzing 115,000 applications, quantified this advantage: hard paywall applications convert at 12.1% compared with 2.1% for freemium apps — a 5.5-fold difference — and generate $3.09 in revenue per install by day 14 compared with $0.38 for freemium apps, an eightfold gap (RevenueCat, 2026).

These numbers are not marginal. They suggest that, for any application that can justify a hard paywall, the revenue case is overwhelming. The question is not whether hard paywalls convert better — they do, by every available metric — but whether the product category supports the necessary pre-purchase expectation of value. Hard paywalls succeed when users arrive at the download decision already understanding what the application does and why they need it. This is true of established brands (the New York Times, Headspace), applications in categories with clear and widely understood value propositions (fitness trackers, language learning), and products that can communicate their benefit through App Store screenshots and descriptions alone.

Voice-to-content applications occupy a different position. The pipeline — record, transcribe, convert — involves three distinct stages, each delivering cumulative value. A user who has never experienced this workflow cannot evaluate the quality of the transcription, the accuracy of the AI-generated artifacts, or the utility of having a spoken thought transformed into a polished email, a structured task list, or a project plan. The value proposition — "speak your thoughts and get back actionable content" — is experientially rich and cannot be conveyed through product page descriptions alone. Numminen et al. (2022) acknowledged this constraint implicitly: their analysis found that paid models work best when consumers can evaluate product quality before purchase, a condition that freemium access directly enables.

Proset therefore rejected the fully locked model, but adopted the mechanism that makes hard paywalls effective: immediate, visible pricing with minimal friction between intent and purchase. The free tier is deliberately tight — 3 transcriptions, 5 conversions, 3-minute recordings — not to be generous, but to deliver exactly one demonstration of value. The onboarding paywall appears immediately after the first transcription completes, while the user's experience of the product's core capability is most vivid. This design borrows the conversion mechanics of a hard paywall (immediate, prominent, low-friction) while preserving the discovery benefit of freemium (experiential evaluation before payment). The result is a hybrid: a soft paywall that behaves like a hard one, converting users at the moment of maximum demonstrated value rather than allowing them to drift indefinitely in a free tier that substitutes for the paid product.

## Credit Card Requirements and AI Pricing: Two Decisions With Asymmetric Evidence

Two additional design decisions required resolution before the revenue funnel could be finalized: whether to require credit card information at the start of a free trial, and whether to adopt usage-based pricing — charging per transcription or per minute of audio — in place of fixed subscription plans. These decisions interact because usage-based models typically require payment information upfront to enable metered billing, while subscription plans can function without it.

The credit card decision involves a well-documented trade-off between conversion rate and top-of-funnel volume. ChartMogul's 2026 analysis of 200 SaaS products found that free trials requiring a credit card convert at 31.4%, while trials without a credit card requirement convert at 8.9% (ChartMogul, 2026). At first glance, the credit card requirement appears nearly four times as effective. However, this metric is misleading in isolation. Totango's longitudinal research on SaaS conversion found that credit card trials achieve an average visit-to-lead conversion rate of approximately 2%, while no-credit-card trials attract a dramatically larger signup pool (Chargebee, 2024). When end-to-end conversion — from visitor to paid subscriber — is calculated, the no-credit-card model produces approximately twice the total paid users. Furthermore, Totango found that no-credit-card trials exhibit superior 90-day retention: 80% compared with 60% for credit card trials (Chargebee, 2024). The users who convert without coercion stay longer.

The mechanism behind this counterintuitive result is consistent with Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory. Requiring a credit card before the user has experienced value frames the trial as a potential loss — the user must remember to cancel to avoid an unwanted charge — which triggers loss aversion and suppresses signup volume. Removing the credit card requirement reframes the trial as a pure gain: the user receives value at no risk. The lower conversion rate per signup is more than compensated by the larger volume of users entering the funnel. For an unknown brand with an experiential product, the no-credit-card approach is the empirically superior choice.

The usage-based pricing decision involves a different calculus, driven by the economics of AI inference. Bessemer Venture Partners' 2026 AI pricing playbook, based on analysis of hundreds of AI-native companies, identified a clear industry trajectory: 67% of SaaS companies now employ usage-based pricing for AI features, and 92% of AI companies using usage-based models have already revised their pricing — indicating that pure usage-based approaches remain unstable and experimentally fluid (Bessemer Venture Partners, 2026). The fundamental challenge is that AI products carry real per-use costs — every transcription, every conversion, every API call consumes compute resources — while consumers demand predictable bills. Lago's 2026 analysis of AI pricing models found that hybrid approaches combining a base subscription with defined usage allowances and overage billing produce the highest net revenue while minimizing customer acquisition friction and billing-related churn (Lago, 2026).

This research directly informed the Proset pricing architecture. Pure usage-based pricing — charging per minute of audio or per artifact generated — was rejected because consumer applications in emerging categories require pricing predictability to overcome adoption barriers. Pure subscription pricing without usage caps was rejected because the AI inference costs are variable and compound across the pipeline: a single recording generates a transcription cost, and each conversion to an artifact type (summary, email, task list, blog post) generates an additional inference cost. A heavy user generating 50 transcriptions and 200 artifact conversions per month would consume disproportionate resources under a flat-rate plan. The hybrid solution embeds usage caps within subscription tiers that account for both pipeline stages: the Free tier allows 3 transcriptions and 5 conversions, Base allows 35 transcriptions and 50 conversions, and Pro allows 70 transcriptions and 150 conversions per month. The cap is the usage limit across the full pipeline; the subscription is the predictable monthly price ($9.99 for Base, $19.99 for Pro). This structure achieves the cost-alignment benefit of usage-based pricing — heavy users either upgrade to Pro or are throttled by their combined transcription and conversion limits — while preserving the consumer psychology benefit of a fixed, known monthly cost. The Pro tier limits were explicitly modeled to ensure profitability even under the 50% early-adopter discount: at an estimated per-transcription cost of $0.01 and per-conversion cost of $0.002, a fully utilized Pro account (70 transcriptions, 150 conversions) costs approximately $1.00 in inference against $10.00 in discounted revenue, yielding a 90% gross margin. Because transcription is automatic with every recording, recording caps are aligned to match: Free allows 3 recordings with 3 transcriptions, Base allows 35 recordings with 35 transcriptions, and Pro allows 70 recordings with 70 transcriptions. If inference costs escalate, the architecture supports adding overage credits to the Pro tier without restructuring the entire model, consistent with Bessemer's (2026) recommendation that AI pricing should start with a hybrid base and add metered components only when cost data justifies them.

## The Free Tier Must Be Tight Enough to Matter

The most consequential error in freemium design is excessive generosity. When free tiers are too feature-rich, users experience no pressure to upgrade because the free product satisfies their needs indefinitely. Shang et al. (2024) demonstrated through fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) that the configuration of free-tier features is a critical determinant of freemium performance, with high-performing apps consistently exhibiting tighter feature restrictions than low-performing competitors. The principle is straightforward: the free tier must solve a real problem, but only the most basic version of it.

Cao et al. (2023) examined the specific challenge of monetizing non-advertising-based applications and found that publishers face critical decisions regarding which features to gate and how aggressively to restrict access. Their analysis revealed that apps employing versioning strategies — offering a functional but deliberately limited free tier — outperform those that provide more generous free access. This finding aligns with the broader literature on consumer decision-making: when a free product is perceived as sufficient, the psychological friction required to initiate a purchase decision is rarely overcome (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979).

The original Proset free tier offered 25 transcriptions, 25 conversions, 5-minute recordings, 500 MB of storage, and file import capabilities — generous by any standard. The redesigned free tier offers 3 transcriptions, 5 conversions, 3-minute recordings, 100 MB of storage, and no file import. Three transcriptions and five conversions are calibrated to deliver one complete experience of the core value proposition: a user records a thought, sees it transcribed, converts the text into several artifact types, and understands what the full pipeline can do. After that, the paywall appears. This is an application of versioning logic validated by Cao et al. (2023) and Shang et al. (2024): the free tier demonstrates the complete pipeline without substituting for the paid product.

## The Purchase Tunnel Must Be Frictionless

Prospect theory, as formulated by Kahneman and Tversky (1979), established that decision-making is reference-dependent and that losses are weighted approximately twice as heavily as equivalent gains. In the context of digital commerce, every additional step between purchase intent and purchase completion is experienced as a friction cost — a loss of time, attention, and cognitive effort that disproportionately reduces conversion probability. When a user selects a paid plan, they are in a high-intent state in which each subsequent screen or form field extinguishes the motivation that brought them to that point.

The original Proset design inserted an Early Bird modal — requesting email confirmation and marketing consent — between plan selection and Stripe checkout. While the modal was thoughtfully designed, it introduced friction at the worst possible moment. Research on default effects provides theoretical support for removing such barriers. Bucher-Koenen et al. (2026) demonstrated that default settings in enrollment contexts increase participation by 23 percentage points, and that the effect is strongest when opting out is simple and the choice architecture is transparent. Extending this logic to the purchase tunnel, the optimal design is one in which completing a purchase is the path of least resistance — discount codes applied automatically rather than requiring active claim behavior.

The redesigned Proset purchase flow eliminates the Early Bird modal entirely. The path from plan selection to Stripe checkout is a single step. The 50% early-adopter discount code is applied automatically at checkout, making the offer visible on the pricing screen rather than gated behind a consent form. This design reflects the finding by Runge et al. (2022) that price promotions in freemium contexts are profitable and do not produce harmful inter-temporal substitution or negative quality inferences. There is no empirical downside to making discounts visible and easy to access.

## The Refund Policy: Why the Platform Defaults Are Optimal

Refund policies in subscription applications present a design tension between revenue protection and consumer trust. The empirical evidence on refund behavior in mobile apps has matured substantially, and the data supports a clear conclusion: restrictive refund policies damage conversion without meaningfully reducing refund rates, while transparent, platform-default policies build the trust necessary for subscription adoption.

Adapty's 2026 analysis of $1.9 billion in revenue across 11,000 applications found that the typical mobile subscription app refund rate falls between 2% and 5% of paid transactions (Adapty, 2026). RevenueCat's State of Subscription Apps report, covering 75,000 applications, confirmed this range (RevenueCat, 2026). Critically, refund rates vary systematically by business model: freemium apps average a 3.4% refund rate, while hard-paywall apps reach 5.8% (Adapty, 2026). The freemium advantage is mechanistically coherent — users who convert after experiencing the product's value are less likely to experience buyer's remorse than users who pay before any hands-on evaluation. This finding reinforces the no-credit-card freemium architecture already adopted by Proset: letting users experience the full pipeline before purchasing not only increases conversion volume, as discussed in Section 2, but also reduces post-purchase regret.

Productivity applications, Proset's category, benefit from a structurally lower refund baseline of 2.5% to 3.5% (Adapty, 2026), compared with 4.7% in health and fitness or 5.1% in education. The mechanism is straightforward: productivity apps solve concrete, observable problems — a user generates a task list and uses it, or converts a spoken idea into a structured document. The value is tangible and immediate, leaving less room for the aspirational disappointment that drives refunds in categories where outcomes are delayed or subjective.

The platform-level refund mechanisms provided by Apple and Google further reduce the need for a custom refund policy. Google Play offers a 48-hour automatic refund window and processes requests within two hours with near-guaranteed approval (Google, 2026). Apple reviews each refund request individually through its Report a Problem portal, with case-by-case evaluation (Apple, 2026). Both platforms refund their service fees on approved refunds, meaning the developer's financial exposure is limited to lost subscription revenue, not platform commissions. These defaults are well-understood by consumers and constitute a de facto industry-standard refund policy that requires no additional implementation.

The decision to adopt these platform defaults rather than imposing additional restrictions — such as no-refund clauses, shortened windows, or manual review gates — is grounded in the broader literature on return-policy leniency. Research from the University of Texas at Dallas found that lenient return policies led to increased purchases overall, with the trust-building effect outweighing the marginal cost of returns (UTD, 2019). In e-commerce contexts, effective return policies significantly enhanced repurchase intention, fostered platform trust, and encouraged positive word-of-mouth (IJEAIS, 2025). The mechanism is consistent with Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory: a clear, fair refund policy frames the purchase as a reversible decision, reducing the perceived risk and lowering the psychological barrier to subscription.

Practically, the Proset refund policy is therefore the platform default: Google Play's 48-hour window and Apple's case-by-case review. A user who subscribes and immediately regrets it can obtain a refund through their respective app store without contacting Proset. This is neither a concession nor a risk — it is the empirically optimal design. The freemium model already ensures that users convert only after experiencing the product's core value, which naturally suppresses refund rates into the 2-3.5% range. Adding friction to the refund process would violate the no-credit-card, low-friction architecture that the rest of the funnel is designed to achieve, and the evidence suggests it would reduce conversion without meaningfully reducing refunds.

## Price Promotions Work and Should Be Salient

Runge et al. (2022) conducted a randomized field experiment with a free-to-play video game publisher, assigning cohorts of users to treatment and control conditions in which promotions for in-game purchases were activated or suppressed. Their results demonstrated that conversion and revenue improved in the treatment group, with no evidence of harmful substitution effects over time. Critically, the authors found no evidence of negative quality inferences from exposure to price variation — users did not perceive discounted products as lower quality. The authors proposed that the zero price of the base product, combined with complementarity between free and premium features, explains why promotions are particularly effective in freemium contexts: the base product creates a reference point of zero cost, against which any premium feature purchase is evaluated purely on its perceived value rather than on its price relative to competitors.

This finding has direct implications for Proset. By making the 50% early-adopter discount visible on the pricing screen and automatically applied at checkout, the application ensures that the promotion is salient and accessible — the two conditions Runge et al. (2022) identified as necessary for promotional effectiveness. The discount is not hidden behind a consent form or email capture; it is a visible, prominent feature of the upgrade experience. This approach leverages the promotional mechanism validated by Runge et al. without introducing the friction that the Kahneman and Tversky (1979) framework predicts would suppress conversion.

## Free Trial Duration Matters, and Shorter Can Be Better

The question of optimal trial duration has received substantial empirical attention. Yoganarasimhan et al. (2023) conducted a large-scale field experiment in partnership with a major SaaS firm, randomly assigning new users to 7-day, 14-day, or 30-day free trials. Their results demonstrated that shorter trials significantly improved subscription outcomes: the 7-day condition produced a 7.27% gain in subscription length over the 30-day baseline, while the 14-day condition produced a 2.59% gain. The mechanism the authors identified is that longer trials create complacency — users delay engagement because they have ample time, and the urgency that drives conversion decisions is diffused across a longer window.

Zhang et al. (2025) extended this work specifically to freemium models, developing a three-stage conversion model to assess the impact of trial duration on free trial adoption, immediate conversion, and delayed conversion. Their large-scale randomized field experiment found that extended trial periods significantly increased free trial adoption and delayed conversion but had no statistically significant effect on immediate conversion. The implication is that longer trials attract more users into the funnel but do not convert them faster — a finding that complicates the intuitive assumption that more time produces more conversions.

For Proset, which operates in a freemium model where users retain indefinite access to basic features, the research suggests that the optimal approach is not a time-limited trial but rather a usage-limited free tier that creates natural conversion pressure through feature exhaustion rather than calendar expiration. This is precisely the approach adopted: rather than offering a 7-day or 30-day trial with full pipeline access, the application offers a permanently free tier with deliberately tight usage limits across both transcription and artifact generation. The conversion trigger is not a calendar deadline but a user's own behavior — when they attempt their fourth transcription, try their sixth artifact conversion, or attempt to access any paid feature, they encounter the paywall at the moment of maximum demonstrated need. This usage-based gating aligns with Zhang et al.'s (2025) finding that product functionality moderates the effect of trial design on conversion.

## The Endowment Effect and Why Engagement Rewards Build Conversion Pressure

Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory established that individuals evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point and that losses from that reference point are weighted more heavily than equivalent gains. Thaler (1980) extended this framework with the endowment effect: the observation that people value goods they already possess more highly than identical goods they do not. In digital products, this manifests as an increasing reluctance to abandon an application the more one has used and invested in it. The sunk-cost effect — the tendency to continue an endeavor once resources have been committed — reinforces this dynamic by making disengagement feel like a loss of accumulated value.

Empirical research on consumer inertia confirms the practical significance of these mechanisms. A CEPR (2024) study of mobile phone subscription choices found that 62% of customers who received personalized information showing they could save money by switching plans did not act on that information, forgoing average savings of £186 per year. Inertia, once established, is remarkably durable and works consistently in favor of the incumbent provider.

Proset was redesigned to leverage these psychological mechanisms through an engagement streak system. Users are shown their current streak of consecutive recording days on the home screen, with milestone rewards at 3, 7, and 30 days. These rewards — bonus transcriptions, additional storage, and achievement recognition — serve a dual purpose. For free users, they extend the trial period slightly, providing just enough additional access to deepen the usage habit. For the application, they create a growing accumulation of user-generated value (recordings, transcriptions, conversions) that makes the prospect of abandoning the platform increasingly costly. When the upgrade prompt eventually appears, the user is not being asked to initiate a subscription; they are being asked to protect an investment they have already made.

## Transparency in Monetization Does Not Reduce Conversion

A persistent concern among application developers is that aggressive monetization will trigger user backlash. The empirical evidence does not support this fear. Bucher-Koenen et al. (2026) demonstrated that the effectiveness of default enrollment settings is strongest when opting out is simple and the choice architecture is transparent, suggesting that users do not resist monetization structures they can clearly understand and control. The key is not avoiding monetization; it is making monetization predictable and escapable.

Cao et al. (2023) found that successful app monetization strategies share a common feature: users understand the boundary between free and paid features before they encounter the paywall. Surprise paywalls — those that appear unexpectedly when a user attempts a specific action — generate frustration. Predictable paywalls — those the user knows about in advance — generate acceptance. This finding informs the Proset design principle that every upgrade prompt must include a visible dismiss option and that the distinction between free and paid features must be communicated clearly before the moment of restriction.

The streak system further supports this principle by creating a reciprocity dynamic: the application gives users bonuses they did not pay for, which makes the eventual monetization ask feel earned rather than extracted. This aligns with the behavioral economics literature on fairness perceptions in exchange relationships, which consistently finds that consumers accept monetization more readily when they perceive the exchange as equitable (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979).

## Conclusion

The Proset revenue funnel is a systematic application of peer-reviewed research to a specific monetization challenge. The decision to reject a fully locked app in favor of a hybrid soft-to-hard paywall reflects Numminen et al.'s (2022) finding that paid models require pre-purchase quality evaluation — a condition the record-transcribe-convert pipeline cannot satisfy from a product page alone — combined with RevenueCat's (2026) empirical demonstration that hard paywall mechanics produce 5.5 times the conversion and 8 times the revenue per install of traditional freemium. The rejection of credit card requirements and pure usage-based pricing reflects ChartMogul's (2026) finding that no-credit-card models produce twice the end-to-end conversion, and Bessemer Venture Partners' (2026) and Lago's (2026) analyses showing that hybrid subscription-with-caps is the stable equilibrium for AI consumer products, particularly those with multi-stage inference costs. The tight free tier reflects Cao et al. (2023) and Shang et al.'s (2024) finding that versioning outperforms generosity, with transcription and conversion caps that allow one complete pipeline experience without substituting for the paid product. The frictionless purchase tunnel applies Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory: every additional step between intent and purchase is a loss that disproportionately reduces conversion. The visible, automatically applied discount leverages Runge et al.'s (2022) demonstration that promotions are profitable and do not harm quality perceptions. The usage-based gating rather than time-based trial reflects Yoganarasimhan et al.'s (2023) finding that shorter trials improve conversion and Zhang et al.'s (2025) extension that trial design interacts with product functionality. The streak engagement system applies Thaler's (1980) endowment effect and the CEPR's (2024) finding that consumer inertia, once established, is durable. And the commitment to transparency reflects Bucher-Koenen et al.'s (2026) demonstration that defaults work best when visible and escapable.

Each element of the redesign is traceable to a specific empirical finding. The application chose its design because the research demanded it, and the research consistently points in one direction: give users a compelling but limited free experience, make the upgrade path frictionless and transparent, apply discounts visibly, and build engagement that makes leaving feel like a loss.
